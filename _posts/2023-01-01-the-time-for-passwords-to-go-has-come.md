The time for passwords to go away has finally come.

### The problem with Passwords:
Passwords have been the traditional method of authentication for decades, but they have proven to be ineffective in providing secure access to sensitive information. Here are some reasons why passwords are bad:
- Passwords are easily guessable: Passwords are often easy to guess because people tend to use simple words or patterns. This makes it easier for attackers to crack them using brute force attacks or dictionary attacks.
- Passwords can be stolen: Passwords can be stolen through phishing scams, keyloggers, or data breaches. Once a password is stolen, the attacker has access to all the accounts that use the same password.
- Passwords are reused: People often reuse passwords across multiple accounts, which is a security risk. If a password is compromised in one account, all other accounts that use the same password are also at risk.
- Passwords can be forgotten: People often forget their passwords, especially if they use different passwords for different accounts. This can lead to frustration and decreased productivity.
- Passwords can be cracked: With the advances in technology, cracking passwords has become easier. Attackers can use powerful computers to crack passwords in a matter of seconds.
In conclusion, passwords are bad because they are easily guessable, can be stolen, are often reused, can be forgotten, and can be cracked. It's important to find a better solution for authentication to ensure the security of sensitive information.

###     The problem with centralized password / credential management systems.
Centralized credential and password management systems are considered bad due to the potential of data breaches leading to sensitive information being compromised. Examples of such breaches include:
- The LinkedIn data breach in 2012 where 167 million email and password combinations were stolen and published online.
- The Yahoo data breach in 2013 where all 3 billion user accounts were compromised, including email addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth, and security questions.
- The Capital One data breach in 2019 where personal information of over 100 million individuals in the US and Canada was stolen, including names, addresses, credit scores, and bank account numbers.
These incidents highlight the vulnerability of centralizing sensitive information and the importance of implementing robust security measures to protect it.
